Most of the people who call us are not trying to get rid of a car for fun. They have just been handed a repair quote, and the number is bigger than they expected. Should they pay it, or sell the car as it is?

As [Christchurch car wreckers](/), we see the same handful of problems over and over in the enquiries we get from Christchurch and Canterbury car owners. Here is what they are, what they usually mean for an older car, and a simple way to make the call.

## The problems that usually end a car's life

### Gearbox, transmission and clutch failures

This is the single most common reason people contact us. Automatic gearboxes that slip, CVTs that shudder, manual clutches that let go, and transfer cases on 4WDs that stop working. Recent examples include a Ford Kuga with a broken transmission that could only just be driven onto a trailer, a 2006 Mazda Axela with transmission problems, a 2014 Volkswagen Golf that wouldn't start and had gearbox faults, and a 2001 Suzuki Aerio whose clutch gave out.

A replacement or rebuilt gearbox, plus the labour to fit it, often costs more than a 10 to 15 year old hatchback is worth.

### Head gaskets, overheating and coolant leaks

A blown head gasket is the classic "is it worth it?" repair. We see them on everything from a 2014 Holden Cruze to a 2001 Nissan Primera with a perfectly straight body. Coolant problems often come first: a leaking water pump on a 2001 Golf, a radiator and coolant leak on a Jaguar, a thermostat that stuck closed on an Audi A3 halfway up the hill to Castle Hill.

If a car has been driven while overheating, the damage can spread well beyond the gasket. Get a proper diagnosis before you commit.

### Cambelts, timing chains and engine failure

When a cambelt snaps or a timing chain jumps on an interference engine, the valves can hit the pistons. That is what we suspect happened to a 2005 Subaru Outback whose cambelt melted, and a 2006 Mercedes SLK 200 that stopped running. Engines that knock and then stop, like a 2012 Subaru Forester we were asked about, often need a full replacement engine.

### Turbos and diesel injection problems

Diesel and turbo petrol cars can be great to own until the turbo fails. A 2014 Mazda Axela 2.2 diesel that still started and drove, but needed a new turbo, is a typical example. On a car with plenty of kilometres, the turbo alone can wipe out most of its value.

### A long list from the WOF inspector

Sometimes there is no single big failure, just a pile of smaller ones. One customer was quoted around $1,400 for four new shocks, a battery and the rest of the WOF list. Others came to us with front struts gone, worn suspension, or chassis rust a garage would not weld. A failed WOF is one of the most common reasons people sell to us.

### Electrical faults and warning lights

Dashboard lights that won't go away, a parking switch fault that flattens the battery every time the car is switched off, starters and alternators that leave the car stranded. These can be cheap fixes or expensive rabbit holes, and diagnosis time alone adds up.

## A simple way to decide: repair or wreck

Before you pay for a big repair, run through these questions.

1. **What is the car worth once it's fixed?** Look up similar cars for sale with the same year, model and kilometres. Be honest about the condition of everything else.
2. **What is the full repair cost?** Ask for a written quote that includes parts, labour and anything the mechanic expects to find once it is apart. Head gasket and gearbox jobs often grow.
3. **What else is due soon?** Tyres, brakes, cambelt, WOF items and rego all count. A car that needs one big repair often needs several small ones too.
4. **Compare the two.** A common rule of thumb: if the repair costs more than about half of what the car would be worth afterwards, selling it as it is usually makes more sense.
5. **How much is your time worth?** Many of the people we buy from have already bought another car. Every week a dead car sits on the lawn, the battery goes flat, tyres go soft and it gets harder to move.

As our [cash for cars](/cash-for-cars/) page puts it, sometimes a $200 part needs $1,000 of labour. That is when a car stops being worth fixing.

## What a wrecker will pay for a broken car

A wrecker values a broken car very differently from a private buyer. We don't need it to pass a WOF, run, or look good. What matters is the vehicle's make, model and condition, and the type of vehicle. Vans, utes and trucks usually earn more because of their larger engines and higher scrap value.

You will usually get a better price if you:

- **Tell us exactly what is wrong.** A mechanic's diagnosis or quote helps us price it accurately.
- **Keep the car complete.** Cars that have had the engine, gearbox or wheels removed are worth less.
- **Have your keys and proof of ownership ready.** We need to confirm the car is yours and has no money owing on it.
- **Don't spend money on it first.** There is no need to replace the battery or tyres just to sell it to us.
